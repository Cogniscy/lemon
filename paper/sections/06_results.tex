\section{Results}
\subsection{From entity overlap to relation factors}
The first WebNLG experiment shows why predicate resources must be explicit. With the initial factor dictionary, only 140 of 308 development edges could be scored, and the missing decomposition rate was 0.546. After rule-based decomposition and cue expansion, all 308 edges had predicate factors and Forward LEMON rose from 0.368 to 0.792. This increase does not mean that all WebNLG texts are faithful. It shows that relation-level diagnosis requires predicate resources before text quality can be evaluated.

Lexical controls identify the added signal. Exact entity-label overlap is already high at 0.922 and does not change under predicate-resource expansion. Token cosine and Jaccard remain fixed because the graph--text strings do not change. LEMON changes because it checks relation roles, predicate meanings, and lexical evidence instead of counting entity mentions alone.

The reverse run checks the same representation from text back to graph. A lexical pseudo-extractor recovered 390 of 408 nodes and 196 of 308 edges from the WebNLG texts. Reverse LEMON reached 0.779, close to the expanded Forward score of 0.792. The MINE-style comparison explains the remaining difficulty: its composite score is 0.803, but the locked 50-fact subset has node information of 0.960 and edge/fact information of only 0.540. The relation is harder to recover than the names of the entities.

A relation-deletion sanity check isolates the same failure mode. Removing relation evidence changes exact entity-label coverage by only 0.002, while Forward LEMON drops by 0.211. Predicate cue coverage and MINE fact recoverability fall by 0.364 and 0.354. Preserving entity names is therefore not the same as preserving the predicate.

\subsection{Cross-domain perturbation sensitivity}
The cross-domain perturbation files test which metrics react when controlled edits damage relation meaning in WebNLG, DrugProt, and BC5CDR. The current inventories audit entity/domain support, predicate cues, participant roles, direction, polarity, and textual evidence. A perturbation can be diagnosed only when the corresponding factor group is present in the inventory.

Table~\ref{tab:perturbation-sensitivity-drop} reports normalized score loss under five perturbations. The columns are different preservation signals rather than one shared accuracy scale: entity recall tracks entity survival; triple match and MINE-style node/edge scoring act as coarse edge/fact alarms; LEMON label-only removes factor decomposition; LEMON is the full weighted factor score.

The table should not be read as a global ranking. Triple match and MINE-style scoring often react more strongly because they treat edge damage as a coarse failure. LEMON is most useful when the reason for failure matters. It reacts to argument swaps and polarity flips when those dimensions are encoded, and it links the response to factor groups that can be inspected.

Table~\ref{tab:ablation-gain} is an internal component analysis, not a comparison with MINE-style or triple matching. Removing participant-role factors produces the largest positive gain for the full model over the ablated variant, so role factors are the largest observed source of diagnostic sensitivity. Evidence, polarity, and direction have smaller effects, mainly in biomedical predicates where these factors are explicit. Label-only scoring can produce a stronger scalar reaction when surface labels disappear, but it cannot say whether the damage concerns roles, direction, polarity, or evidence.



\subsection{Pilot expert trace review}\label{sec:expert-trace-review}
The expert review directly addresses whether LEMON's factor-level traces agree with human assessments of relation failure. Four expert reviewers completed the form, yielding 140 expert--factor judgments over 35 factor rows. LEMON reached 85.0\% exact agreement with expert labels (119/140; Wilson 95\% CI: 78.2--90.0\%) and 85.8\% weighted exact agreement. Chance-corrected agreement was substantial: pooled Cohen's $\kappa=0.791$, Fleiss' inter-expert $\kappa=0.689$, and Krippendorff's ordinal $\alpha=0.871$. Experts formed a $\geq$3/4 majority on 28 rows; LEMON matched that majority on 27 rows (96.4\%), and every LEMON label was supported by at least one expert. Disagreements concentrated in relation-blur and relation-missing cases, especially predicate-meaning, direction, and evidence factors.

\subsection{LLM factor judging as a complementary view}
The LLM probe evaluates semantic recoverability rather than rule-based perturbation labels. Each judge saw the source edge, the factor inventory, and the perturbed text, then labeled each factor as covered, partial, or absent. The mean LLM factor score is 0.809, pairwise agreement is 0.597, and agreement with rule-based perturbation labels is 0.561. These values indicate moderate model-judge stability. Rule-based perturbations record intended damage; LLM judges estimate how much of the original factor remains recoverable from context. The expert review above tests direct agreement with human factor labels, while model-based judging shows which failures remain inferable.
