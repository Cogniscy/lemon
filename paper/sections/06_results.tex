\section{Results}
\subsection{From entity overlap to relation factors}
The first WebNLG pass shows why predicate resources matter before an evaluation metric can be trusted. With the initial factor dictionary, only 140 of 308 development edges could be scored, and the missing decomposition rate was 0.5455. After deterministic decomposition and cue expansion, all 308 edges had predicate factors and Forward \lemonscore{} rose from 0.3683 to 0.7915. This increase is not a claim that all WebNLG texts are faithful. It shows that factor-level evaluation requires explicit predicate resources before it can diagnose text quality.

Lexical controls isolate what the factor score adds. Exact entity-label overlap is already high at 0.9215 and does not change under predicate-resource expansion. Token cosine and Jaccard are also fixed because the underlying graph--text strings do not change. The factor score changes because it checks relation roles, predicate meanings, and lexical evidence. The added signal is therefore not a different way of counting entity mentions; it is a relation-level check that becomes available once predicates have decompositions.

The reverse run gives a second check on the same representation. A deterministic lexical pseudo-extractor recovered 390 of 408 nodes and 196 of 308 edges from the WebNLG texts. Reverse \lemonscore{} reached 0.7789, close to the expanded Forward score of 0.7915. This does not evaluate a general-purpose extraction system; it shows that the same predicate factors can be used coherently in both directions.

The MINE-style comparison explains why this matters. Its deterministic node/edge composite is 0.8028, but the aggregate is dominated by node recovery. On the locked 50-fact subset, node information is 0.9600, while edge information and deterministic fact recoverability are both 0.5400. A separate LLM fact check gives a close score of 0.5200 and agrees with the deterministic fact decision on 0.9800 of cases. The consistent pattern is that entity mentions are easier to preserve and recover than the relations that connect them.

A relation-deletion sanity check makes the same point more directly. Removing relation evidence changes exact entity-label coverage by only -0.0020, while Forward \lemonscore{} drops by -0.2106. Predicate cue coverage and MINE fact recoverability fall by -0.3636 and -0.3539. Preserving the entities is therefore not the same as preserving the predicate; relation-aware scores are needed to see this failure mode.

\subsection{Cross-domain perturbation sensitivity}
The cross-domain perturbation files ask which metrics react when controlled edits damage relation meaning in WebNLG, DrugProt, and BC5CDR. Table~\ref{tab:perturbation-sensitivity-drop} reports mean score drops. Larger values mean stronger sensitivity to the perturbation. Small values are not missing computations; they indicate that the metric is insensitive to that kind of semantic damage.

The aggregate pattern is interpretable. Entity recall drops least because entity strings often remain even when the relation is damaged. Triple-style and node/edge scores react more strongly because they require relation or edge evidence, but they collapse the error into one coarse decision. \lemonscore{} is less useful as a single harshness number than as a diagnostic decomposition: it reacts to argument swap and polarity flip when those dimensions are encoded in the inventory, and it remains quiet when a semantic dimension is absent from the factor schema. This is the intended behavior. The metric can diagnose only those predicate dimensions that the inventory makes explicit.

\input{tables/table_ablation_gain}

The ablation results show which parts of the factor representation drive sensitivity. Removing participant-role factors produces the largest positive gain for the full model over the ablated variant, so roles are the main source of sensitivity to argument and node-level damage. Removing evidence, polarity, or direction has smaller but interpretable effects, mostly in biomedical predicates where these factors are explicit. Label-only scoring can produce a larger scalar drop when surface labels disappear, but it cannot say whether the damage concerns roles, direction, polarity, or evidence. The full factor score is therefore more diagnostic, even when it is not always the largest scalar response.

\subsection{LLM factor judging as a complementary view}
The LLM probe evaluates semantic recoverability rather than deterministic damage. Each judge saw the source edge, factor inventory, and perturbed text, then labeled each factor as covered, partial, or absent. Table~\ref{tab:llm-reliability} summarizes the three judge batches.

\input{tables/table_llm_reliability}

The mean LLM factor score is 0.809, pairwise agreement on overlapping factor judgments is 0.597, and agreement with deterministic perturbation labels is 0.561. The moderate agreement is informative. The deterministic perturbation layer records intended damage introduced by a controlled edit. The LLM judges estimate how much of the original factor remains recoverable from residual context. The two signals therefore answer different questions: one provides auditable stress tests, while the other reveals when context still makes a damaged relation inferable. This distinction is important for interpreting the method. \lemonscore{} exposes relation-level failure modes; model-based judging can help decide which of those failures remain semantically recoverable.
