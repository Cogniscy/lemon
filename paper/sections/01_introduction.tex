
\section{Introduction}
A graph edge is small, but it carries more than two names. In an edge \((s,p,o)\), the predicate \(p\) fixes the relation between the arguments: who did what, to whom, in which direction, and with which semantic commitment. If a graph-to-text system mentions the entities but loses the predicate, the sentence may still look fluent while no longer saying the same thing. The reverse problem is similar: a reconstructed graph is useful only if it recovers the relation, not merely the endpoints.

This failure is easy to overlook. A sentence can mention \textit{Alan Bean} and \textit{Wheeler, Texas} without stating that Wheeler is his place of birth. A biomedical abstract can preserve the same chemical and protein names while turning inhibition into activation. Standard WebNLG evaluation uses reference metrics such as BLEU, METEOR, chrF++, TER, BERTScore, and BLEURT, together with human criteria such as data coverage, relevance, correctness, and fluency \cite{gardent2017webnlg,castroferreira2020webnlg,gehrmann2021gem}. These signals remain useful, but they rarely identify which part of a predicate was preserved, weakened, or lost.

\lemonscore{} treats the predicate as the unit that must be audited. Each predicate is decomposed into typed, weighted semantic factors. For example, \textit{birthPlace} contains a person-like subject, a biographical relation, and a place-like object. A DrugProt inhibition predicate contains a chemical actor, a gene/protein target, negative regulation, directionality, and evidence form. The score asks whether these factors are expressed in text or recovered in a reconstructed graph, and records which factors are missing.

The setting is deliberately restricted. We do not attempt general text--text similarity or open-ended semantic parsing. The source graph supplies the initial semantic structure, and \lemonscore{} audits the predicate layers that should remain stable across graph and text. In this sense, the method is a controlled graph--text instance of a broader layered-semantics idea: represent relation meaning explicitly, then compare it through interpretable parts rather than through a single opaque similarity score.

The framework is bidirectional. In KG$\rightarrow$Text, \lemonscore{} evaluates whether the text covers the semantic factors of the input graph. In Text$\rightarrow$KG, it evaluates whether a reconstructed graph preserves the source predicate factors. We also implement a \minebase{} node/edge baseline inspired by KGGen's Measure of Information in Nodes and Edges (MINE), which evaluates text-to-KG information retention through recovered nodes and relations \cite{mo2025kggen}. This baseline is a comparison point, not a reproduction of the full KGGen benchmark.

The experiments follow one line of reasoning: factor coverage beyond entity overlap on WebNLG, reuse of the same factors in the reverse direction, controlled perturbations for intended semantic damage, transfer of the inventory format to DrugProt and BC5CDR, a pilot four-expert trace review, and a small three-judge LLM probe for residual recoverability.

The contributions are a bidirectional factor-level formulation for graph--text semantic alignment; fixed factor inventories for WebNLG, DrugProt, and BC5CDR; reproducible forward, reverse, perturbation, and ablation analyses; a MINE-style node/edge comparison; a pilot expert trace review; and an exploratory LLM reliability probe. The paper is diagnostic rather than leaderboard-oriented: it shows when entity, triple, node/edge, vector-space, and factor-level views disagree, and what those disagreements reveal about relation-level semantic preservation.
