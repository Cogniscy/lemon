\section{Introduction}
Knowledge graphs express information through typed relations. In an edge \((s,p,o)\), the predicate \(p\) is not a decorative label between two entity names: it determines the roles of the arguments and the relation that should be preserved in text. The same problem appears in reverse. If a verbalization is converted back into a graph, a useful reconstructed edge must recover the predicate meaning, not only the two entities.

This distinction is easy to miss in graph--text evaluation. A sentence can mention \textit{Alan Bean} and \textit{Wheeler, Texas} without expressing that Wheeler is his place of birth. A biomedical abstract can contain the same chemical and protein names while changing inhibition into activation. Standard WebNLG evaluation uses reference metrics such as BLEU, METEOR, chrF++, TER, BERTScore, and BLEURT, as well as human criteria such as data coverage, relevance, correctness, and fluency \cite{gardent2017webnlg,castroferreira2020webnlg,gehrmann2021gem}. These signals are useful, but they usually do not say which part of a predicate was preserved, weakened, or lost.

We address this gap with \lemonscore{}, a source-driven diagnostic framework for graph--text alignment. The method decomposes each predicate into typed, weighted semantic factors. For example, \textit{birthPlace} includes a person-like subject, a biographical relation, and a place-like object. A DrugProt inhibition predicate includes a chemical actor, a gene/protein target, negative regulation, directionality, and evidence form. The score asks whether these factors are expressed in text or recovered in a reconstructed graph. It therefore gives partial credit when entities survive but relation meaning does not, and it produces a trace of the missing factors.

The framework is bidirectional. In the forward direction, KG$\rightarrow$Text, \lemonscore{} evaluates whether the text covers the semantic factors of the input graph. In the reverse direction, Text$\rightarrow$KG, it evaluates whether a reconstructed graph preserves the source predicate factors. We also implement a \minebase{} node/edge baseline inspired by KGGen's Measure of Information in Nodes and Edges (MINE), which evaluates text-to-KG information retention through recovered nodes and relations \cite{mo2025kggen}. This baseline is used as a transparent comparison point, not as a reproduction of the full KGGen benchmark.

The paper asks six research questions. \textbf{RQ1:} Does factor-level coverage capture graph-to-text semantic preservation beyond entity-label overlap? \textbf{RQ2:} Does the same factor representation support both forward coverage and reverse recoverability? \textbf{RQ3:} How does factor-level recoverability relate to node/edge information retention in a MINE-style baseline? \textbf{RQ4:} Do the metrics behave predictably under meaning-destroying and meaning-preserving perturbations? \textbf{RQ5:} Does the deterministic factor format produce useful perturbation diagnostics on biomedical graph--text data? \textbf{RQ6:} How do independent LLM factor judges compare with deterministic perturbation labels?

The contributions follow from this progression: a bidirectional factor-level formulation for graph--text semantic alignment; fixed factor inventories for WebNLG, DrugProt, and BC5CDR; reproducible forward, reverse, perturbation, and ablation analyses; a MINE-style node/edge comparison; and an exploratory three-judge LLM reliability probe. The study is diagnostic rather than leaderboard-oriented. Its purpose is to show when entity, triple, node/edge, and factor-level views disagree, and what those disagreements reveal about relation-level semantic preservation.
