\section{Introduction}
Knowledge graphs encode facts as typed relations between entities. In RDF-style graph-to-text generation, a system is given triples such as \((s,p,o)\) and must produce a natural-language verbalization that is fluent and faithful to the source graph. The central evaluation problem is therefore not only whether the generated sentence contains the right entity names, but whether it preserves the meaning of each predicate and the roles implied by that predicate.

This distinction matters because predicate meaning is rarely monolithic. A relation such as \textit{birthPlace} invokes at least a biographical event, a person-like subject, and a location-like object. A relation such as \textit{leaderTitle} invokes political authority, an office or title, and often a governed place or institution. A generated text may mention both entities while omitting the relation, may express the relation through paraphrase, or may verbalize only one role of the predicate. Holistic metrics collapse these cases into a single sentence-level score.

The WebNLG line of shared tasks established a widely used benchmark for mapping sets of RDF triples to text \cite{gardent2017webnlg,castroferreira2020webnlg}. Its evaluation practice combines automatic generation metrics, such as BLEU, METEOR, chrF++, TER, BERTScore, and BLEURT, with human criteria such as data coverage, relevance, correctness, and fluency. These metrics and criteria are valuable, but they do not provide a within-predicate audit trail: they do not say which semantic component of which source predicate was preserved, omitted, or only weakly expressed.

We propose \lemonscore{}, an interpretable, source-directed coverage framework. Instead of decomposing generated text into claims, \lemonscore{} decomposes graph predicates into weighted semantic factors. Each factor has a role, such as \textit{predicate meaning}, \textit{subject domain}, or \textit{object domain}, and a salience weight. Coverage is computed by checking evidence for each factor in the generated text. The result is both a score and a diagnostic explanation at the edge and factor level.

This paper reports a reproducible pilot on a stratified WebNLG subset. The pilot has four contributions. First, it defines a controlled factor schema for RDF predicates grounded in predicate-role decomposition. Second, it proposes a weighted factor-level coverage score for graph--text evaluation. Third, it implements a deterministic coverage baseline with explicit lexical evidence and audit trails. Fourth, it reports an error-driven expansion experiment showing that missing predicate decompositions, not just text quality, strongly affect factor-level coverage.

The scope is deliberately limited. Predicate decompositions are initially bootstrapped by rules and synthetic LLM adjudication rather than by human domain experts. The coverage scorer is lexical and deterministic rather than neural. These design choices make the first pilot reproducible and inspectable, but they do not constitute human validation.
