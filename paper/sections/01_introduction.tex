\section{Introduction}
Knowledge graphs encode information as typed relations between entities. In RDF-style graph-to-text generation, an input edge \((s,p,o)\) is not only a pair of entity names. The predicate \(p\) states which relation holds, which semantic roles the arguments fill, and which part of the meaning must be preserved in text. The same issue appears in the reverse direction. If a text is converted back into a graph, a useful reconstructed edge must preserve relation meaning, not merely mention the same entities.

This distinction is often hidden by holistic automatic metrics. A sentence may mention \textit{Alan Bean} and \textit{Wheeler, Texas} while omitting that the relation is \textit{birthPlace}. It may paraphrase the relation, replace it with a weaker predicate, or preserve only the subject and object. Standard WebNLG evaluation uses reference metrics such as BLEU, METEOR, chrF++, TER, BERTScore, and BLEURT, together with human criteria such as data coverage, relevance, correctness, and fluency \cite{gardent2017webnlg,castroferreira2020webnlg,gehrmann2021gem}. These criteria are useful, but they do not say which internal semantic component of a predicate is covered or missing.

We propose \lemonscore{}, a source-driven diagnostic framework for graph--text semantic alignment. The core idea is to decompose each source predicate into typed, weighted semantic factors. For example, \textit{birthPlace} can be represented by a person-like subject factor, a biographical-relation factor, and a place-like object factor. \lemonscore{} then checks whether these factors are expressed in text or recovered in a reconstructed graph. This creates a diagnostic layer between binary triple-level metrics, such as FactSpotter, and text-driven atomic-fact metrics, such as FActScore \cite{zhang2023factspotter,min2023factscore}.

The framework is bidirectional. In the forward direction, KG$\rightarrow$Text, it measures whether a text covers the semantic factors of the input graph. In the reverse direction, Text$\rightarrow$KG, it measures whether a reconstructed graph preserves the semantic factors of the source graph. We also implement a \minebase{} node/edge baseline inspired by KGGen's Measure of Information in Nodes and Edges (MINE), which evaluates text-to-KG information retention through recovered nodes and relations \cite{mo2025kggen}. The baseline is used as a transparent comparison point; it is not a reproduction of the full KGGen/MINE benchmark.

The paper asks four research questions. \textbf{RQ1:} Does factor-level coverage capture graph-to-text semantic preservation beyond entity-label overlap? \textbf{RQ2:} Does the same factor representation support both forward coverage and reverse recoverability? \textbf{RQ3:} How does factor-level recoverability relate to node/edge information retention in a MINE-style baseline? \textbf{RQ4:} Do the metrics behave predictably under meaning-destroying and meaning-preserving perturbations?

The contributions are: (1) a bidirectional, weighted factor-level formulation for graph--text semantic alignment; (2) a reproducible WebNLG pilot with factor decomposition resources, forward and reverse scores, and audit trails; (3) a MINE-style node/edge information baseline adapted to WebNLG graph--text pairs; and (4) controlled perturbation calibration showing that relation-level damage is detected by factor-aware metrics while simple entity-label overlap remains nearly unchanged. The study is deliberately framed as a pilot. Factor decompositions are bootstrapped with rules and synthetic LLM adjudication, and the resulting resource is not claimed to be human-validated gold.
