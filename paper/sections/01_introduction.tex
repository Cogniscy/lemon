\section{Introduction}

Knowledge graphs encode facts as typed relations between entities. In graph-to-text and RDF-to-text generation, a system must verbalize these relations while preserving the meaning of the source graph. A sentence that mentions the subject and object labels may still fail to express the intended predicate: for example, a text may contain both a person and a city name while omitting whether the city is a birthplace, residence, death place, or headquarters.

The dominant WebNLG evaluation setup compares generated texts against references using automatic metrics such as BLEU, METEOR, chrF++, TER, BERTScore, and BLEURT, and supplements them with human judgments over data coverage, relevance, correctness, and fluency~\cite{gardent2017webnlg,castroferreira2020webnlg,zhang2020bertscore,sellam2020bleurt}. These metrics are useful for benchmarking generation quality, but they are not designed to explain which semantic component of a source predicate was omitted or paraphrased. Even graph-to-text factuality metrics such as FactSpotter decide whether a complete triple is present in text, without decomposing the predicate into internal semantic roles~\cite{zhang2023factspotter}.

We propose LEMON-Factor, an interpretable metric layer for graph--text meaning preservation. The central idea is source-directed decomposition: each graph predicate is represented as a weighted set of semantic factors, such as relation type, subject domain, object domain, location, time, membership, or biographical role. A text is then scored by the extent to which it provides lexical or semantic evidence for each factor. This produces both a scalar score and an audit trail indicating which factor was covered or missed.

The present paper reports a pilot implementation on WebNLG. We convert WebNLG triples and references to a unified GraphText representation, derive a factor inventory from train-side predicates, construct seed and synthetic LLM-adjudicated decompositions, and evaluate deterministic graph--text coverage on a stratified development subset. We also run error-driven expansion of missing decompositions and lexical cues, showing how the metric can diagnose and reduce coverage blind spots.

Our contributions are: (i) a role-aware factor schema for RDF predicate decompositions; (ii) a weighted coverage scoring procedure for source graph predicates against text; (iii) a reproducible WebNLG pilot with before/after coverage expansion and baseline comparison tables; and (iv) a limitation-aware protocol that distinguishes rule-based, synthetic LLM-adjudicated, and future human-validated references.
